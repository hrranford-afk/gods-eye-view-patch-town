# Flow pass (`islands_look.html?flow=1`)

URL switch that adds a tidal current to the sea. It works with `?pass=landing`, with the default scene, with `?sea=2` (it implies the sea=2 look and replaces its shader with a flow-aware copy), with `?q=low` and with `?post=0`. With `?flow` absent nothing changes: default hash `8d962c0c`, 88,790 triangles, `sea-test.js` and `look-test.js` unchanged.

Shots (regenerate with `node tests/flow-render.js`): `shots/flow-1.png` wide view, `shots/flow-2.png` close shore at high tide (wrack line), `shots/flow-3.png` the same shore at low tide, `shots/flow-arrows.png` and `shots/flow-arrows-ebb.png` arrow overlay at mid tide, rising (flood) and falling (ebb). Test: `tests/flow-test.js` (26 checks, all passing). `tests/flow-peek.js` is a small dev helper that frames one kind of flotsam.

Extra parameters: `ebb=1` makes a manually set tide level count as falling instead of rising (`__li.setTideDir(-1)` does the same at run time), `flowdbg=1` shows the debug panel, `arrows=1` turns the arrow overlay on (it can also be toggled in the panel). `seat=` still freezes the sea clock.

## What this is, and what it is not
It is a **simple potential-flow picture of a tidal stream, not a hydrodynamic simulation.** There is no pressure, no mass of water, no bed friction model, no waves driving current, no free surface. The current is an analytic formula evaluated on request, scaled by the tide phase, so it reverses cleanly and costs almost nothing. It looks right at the scale of a table-top diorama (water speeds up past headlands, slows in the lee, curls round island ends, turns at slack water) but it would not predict where a real boat would drift.

## 1 The flow field (`__li.flowAt(x, z, t)`, also `__li.flow.at`)
Everything is seeded (`seed` 1122 gives axis 0.90 rad, about 52 degrees from +x) and everything is a pure function of `(x, z, T)`, `T` being tide time in seconds (one tide cycle is 55 s, as the Animate button already uses). It returns `[u, v]` in metres per second (visual game metres: peak about 1.1 m/s in open water, about 2.2 m/s beside an island).

Pieces, all plain numbers in `__li.flow.params()` / `__li.flow.dump()` (a 635 character JSON string, version 1, that can be saved and reloaded):
- Tidal stream `c(T) * U0` along one seeded axis, `c = cos(2 pi T / 55)`. Level is `A sin(2 pi T / 55)` (A = 1.01 m, the page's tide), so the stream is strongest at mid tide, zero at high and low water, flood while the level rises, ebb while it falls.
- One doublet (flow past a cylinder) per island, radius from the island's size (the crescent harbour island is one disc 66% of its outer radius). This gives acceleration beside islands, a stagnation point upstream, slow water in the lee, and a speed-up in a gap between two islands because the side velocities add.
- One compact circulation ring per island, random sign and strength 0.3 to 0.65 of the stream, scaled by `c`. This curls the stream round island ends.
- A pair of counter-rotating lee eddies on the downstream side of each island, one pair for the flood side and one for the ebb side, each scaled by `max(c, 0)` or `max(-c, 0)` (so the eddies move to the other end of the island at the turn of the tide). Compact vortices, so they add no far field.
- A weak steady residual (0.22 m/s mean drift plus a gentle gyre from the curl of a seeded stream function, stored on a 65 x 65 grid). This is what makes things leave the table eventually, as a real tidal sea has a net drift.
- Shallow water: speed is multiplied by 0.25 to 1 as the depth under the surface falls from 1.2 m to 0 (read from the height grid and the tide), and the stream is masked out inside each island disc. This part is the only thing that is not a potential flow.

Everything except the last item is divergence free, and the test checks it: mean |div| / mean |grad u| is 1.8 percent over 1,044 open-water points (max 0.047 per second, which is finite-difference and bilinear grid noise, not a source), and the net flux through 4 m circles in open water is under 1.2 percent of the through-flow. The stream along the axis reverses (mean +1.21 m/s flood, -1.09 m/s ebb) and is 0.21 m/s at slack against 1.12 m/s at mid tide at a open-sea point.

Manual tide: the slider gives a level, not a time, so `flowTime()` picks the phase on the rising branch (or the falling branch with `ebb=1` / `setTideDir(-1)`). Two whole cycles (110 s) are added so the drift has already spread when the page opens. In Animate mode the phase is the page's own tide clock.

## 2 The sea shader (`SEAF_FS`, a copy of the sea-pass shader with changes)
A 64 x 64 RGBA8 texture is rebuilt in JavaScript whenever the tide phase changes: velocity (R, G; plus or minus 3 m/s), convergence (B) and vorticity (A), both plus or minus 0.5 per second, from central differences of the same field. One texel fetch gives the local current.
- Two-phase flow map (cycle 7 s, weights `1 - |1 - 2 phase|`, re-normalised so the cross-fade does not dim the pattern): the chop noise, the foam-streak noise and the glitter facet cells are looked up at `p - u * tau * phase` for two offset phases and cross-faded. They are carried by the stream, bend round the islands and re-form at each reset. Two of the ripple trains (1.31 m and 0.71 m) are done the same way, the two finest get half the displacement, the long swells and the 9 m / 4.7 m / 2.45 m trains are left alone.
- Breaking the combing, in order of effect: (a) isotropic chop from the existing noise map, in addition to the wind-stretched chop; (b) the wind direction changes slowly by region (about +-0.4 rad, from two low-frequency taps), so the fan of trains is not parallel everywhere; (c) each train has its own patchy amplitude, so a train fades in and out across the sea; (d) a bigger domain warp (11 m) plus a fine one (1.8 m); (e) one more train at a steeper angle to the wind; (f) a new noise texture (`makeFlowNoise`, 256 x 256, 8, 6 and 5 base cells, 4 octaves) for streaks, because the old map has only 3 x 3 cells and made a visibly repeating stripe pattern.
- Foam streaks and slicks: noise stretched along the tidal axis (16 to 20 to 1), carried by the flow and warped, so streaks bend round islands. They are broken up with a second slow noise, fade at slack water, are brightened by convergence and shear (from the texture) and collect in slow eddies. Slicks are wide calm bands (ripple amplitude down by up to 55 percent, slightly lighter and smoother water). Also a second lee test: land up-current of a pixel calms it, like the wind lee.
- The stretch is along the global tidal axis, not the local flow direction. A per-pixel direction rotates the sampling coordinates by metres times radians and shreds the pattern; carrying the axis-aligned pattern by the real displacement bends it where the flow bends and keeps it clean. The cost is that eddies do not make true spiral streaks: they swirl the pattern only as far as 7 s of displacement allows.

## 3 Drifting flotsam
Up to 260 items (120 on Low) in 5 instanced meshes, 5 draw calls: pale cuttlebone-like shells, weathered driftwood with a branch stub, kelp strands (three fronds and bladders), a few crates, a very few green bottles (original shapes and colours, no branding). Sizes are exaggerated about 3 to 4 times so they read at table-top distance.
- Simulation: fixed 0.5 s steps of tide time, Euler, per item `v = flowAt + leeway`. Leeway is a small wind term for wood, crates and bottles. Near a shore on the flood an onshore swash term pushes items up the slope (strong on a gentle slope, weak on a steep one). An item never enters land: it slides along the shore or stays. It is fully deterministic: each item has its own seeded generator, a checkpoint is kept every 55 s of tide time and any `T` is reached by restoring the last checkpoint and stepping forward, so `__li.flow.positions(T)` returns the same list for the same seed and `T`, in any order of calls (test: identical on two page loads and after running on to T=300 and jumping back to T=150).
- Beaching: only when the tide is above 0.80 m (about the top 20 percent of the cycle), the item is in under 0.12 m of water and the slope is under 0.45 (gentle shore). It is then carried up to a strandline height of about 0.93 m (the highest tide) plus or minus 0.06, spread along the shore by up to 1.6 m so items do not stack. The test confirms every first beaching is at a tide above 0.80 m and every beached item lies at terrain height 0.63 to 1.21 m, i.e. dry at low tide. Beached items lie still (no bob) with wood and kelp turned along the shore. After 150 s of tide time (nearly three cycles) a beached item is collected by the next spring tide and respawns (counted).
- Leaving: an item reaching the table edge respawns on the upwind rim (counted). Counters only, no per-item tracking: `__li.flow.counts()` returns `{total, floating, beached, lostRespawned, beachRecycled, beachings}`; the test shows 84 lost and respawned and 96 recycled in 400 s of tide time (about 7 tide cycles) of 260 items, with up to 78 on the shore at once and 133 beaching events.
- Drawing: floating items sit on the surface and bob and roll (cosmetic, driven by the sea clock, which `seat=` freezes); crates and wood ride low in the water. Beached items follow the ground.

## 4 Boats
The sailing boat (the moored one in the crescent harbour, circling at r = 4.6 m) reads the stream under it: its angular speed is multiplied by `1 + 0.2 * (u along track)` and it is set sideways by 2.2 times the cross-track component, smoothed. The test sees an along-track component of about -0.5 m/s and a set of 0.25 m. Wake blobs are carried with the local current so wakes drift off sideways instead of staying put. Only this boat moves, so only this boat is affected; the rowing boat on the shingle and the moored boats keep their tide-bob.

## 5 Debug panel
Off by default. `?flowdbg=1` adds a small panel (tide time, flood / slack / ebb, floating, beached, lost and respawned, recycled, total beachings) with an arrows toggle; `?arrows=1` turns the overlay on without the panel. The overlay is a 2D canvas: arrows on a 9.5 m grid over open water, length and colour by speed (cyan slow, orange fast), dashed circles are the island discs the field uses.

## Cost (software GL only, so no fps claim)
- Triangles: landing + flow is 137,669 (landing alone 124,477; the flotsam adds 13,192; budget 150k). Low: 102,559. Default scene + flow: 101,982. Default hash unchanged without `?flow`.
- JavaScript per frame: the 64 x 64 texture rebuild (4,096 evaluations of the field, only when the phase changes) plus one matrix write per item. Sim step 260 items every 0.5 s of tide time. The first frame runs the sim from T=0 to about 110 s: 220 steps, a few tens of milliseconds.
- Fragment shader, High: about 45 texture taps per water pixel against 21 for sea=2 (flow 1, current lee 2, wind angle 2, warps 3, patches 2, streak 8, chop 8, plus the old ones), 10 ripple evaluations against 8. Low: no current lee, no slick, no breaks, 1 stretched + 1 isotropic chop tap per layer, 5 ripple trains: about 25 taps. This is roughly twice the cost of sea=2 per water pixel. Estimate only; not measured on a device. If it is too heavy, drop the isotropic chop second octave, the slick, and the glitter layer cross-fade first.

## How much did the "computer made" feeling drop (honest)
Looking at `sea-close.png` (sea=2) against the same view with `?flow=1`, and `sea-wide.png` against `flow-1.png`:
- Gone or much reduced: the long parallel combed ripples; the repeated white dashes (they were cut in a regular grid); the sea looking frozen (patterns now move with the stream and reverse with the tide); the empty flat sea between islands (now has streaks, slicks, small eddies and drifting objects).
- Still there: the new isotropic chop reads as slightly crinkled foil close up, with squiggly glitter; foam streaks are still a little too evenly sized in the wide view (some "slashes" in rows) because they come from one noise texture with a 2 m tile across the stream; there is still no real wave breaking, no long swell lines, no spray; the current does not visibly curl into spirals around the eddies.
- My judgement from the images, not a blind test: about a third to a half of the artificial look is gone in the wide and middle views, much less in a very close view. The single biggest remaining giveaway is now the foam streak pattern repeating, and the sea colour still being flat in large patches.

## Remaining gaps
1. The field is islands as discs plus noise. Real land shape (the crescent harbour, the sand bars, the skerry) only enters through the shallow-water slowdown and the beaching rule, so channels between close islands are not as narrow in the flow as they look. Replace discs with a distance-field-based potential (solve Laplace on the 64 grid, a few hundred iterations, once) if this matters.
2. No river or wind-driven component, no waves refracting round headlands (the swell trains ignore land and flow, except the two advected ones).
3. Shells and kelp drift as surface objects; in life shells sink. The pale shells are cuttlebone-like. Items are oversized.
4. Rocky shores: items slide along them and never beach, so rocks show no wrack. Only gentle shingle and sand collects it.
5. Respawn is on the rim upwind, so the supply is uniform; no spawning from rivers, wrecks or the player's boat.
6. Foam streak repetition (see above) and the flow-map cross-fade pulsing: fine ripple trains lose amplitude mid-cycle where the current is fast (partly hidden by re-normalising and by noise).
7. The slider has no time, so manual tide picks a phase; scrubbing the slider back and forth goes through the same phase each time. The seeded drift does not match what the water did before you moved it.

## How it could feed gameplay later
The same `flowAt(x, z, T)` is a pure function of world position and game time with no randomness at call time, so a headless simulation can use it, and a save only needs `{seed, flow params, T}`.
- Boat travel time: integrate boat position as `v = heading * speed + flowAt`. A 1.5 mile trip would take visibly longer against the flood than with it; the ledger event that books the trip can store the departure phase and the computed time, and a replay gives the same arrival.
- Fishing and tide windows: eddies and slicks (vorticity and convergence are already in the texture) mark where plankton and fish collect; a fishing spot's yield can read `abs(vort)` and `conv` at the spot, and slack water at high or low tide is a natural quiet window to land or set a net, matching the contract's tide-window rule.
- Salvage finds in the ledger: a beaching event is `{key: seed + item id + beaching count, kind, x, z, T}`. The key makes it idempotent (a save and reload in the middle of a tide adds the item once). A beached item becomes a unit with one location (shore, hand, stores); the existing respawn / recycle counters become the "uncollected wrack that the spring tide takes back". Items found today are exactly the ones that `positions(T)` says are beached at `T`, so the same seed gives the same finds, and "go and check the north shore after the next high tide" is an honest plan.
- Danger and cargo: a loose crate or boat adrift is a unit that follows the same integration, so "it will be on that beach at the next high tide" is something a player can reason about and a scout can report late (observation time against delivery time, section 7 of the world contract).
- Not gameplay-ready: item counts, speeds and the 55 s tide are visual tuning, not game-time values. Real minutes per tide, real drift speeds and a rule for what a beached item is worth are all open (TUNING, as the contract says).
