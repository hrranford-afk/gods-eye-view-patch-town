# Rivers, lakes and the water cycle for bigger islands (research note, 24 Nov 2026)

Status: research for the "bigger islands with rivers, lakes and a full water cycle" idea (EVOLUTION_TREE.md, last section; owner answers 24 Nov: same game, you sail to them; you can build on rivers later; floods matter, gently). Companion code: `landform/hydrology.py` (numpy only), `landform/test_hydrology.py` (61 checks, all pass), `landform/HYDROLOGY_REPORT.md` (tables and maps for the 12 exported worlds plus an invented bigger island). Tags: VERIFIED (a source this session or a standard textbook relation), PLAUSIBLE (consistent with sources, not pinned), UNVERIFIED, TUNING (our number, chosen to look right). Wikipedia was not reachable from this session, so textbook relations marked "from memory" were not re-checked against a page.

## 0. One-page summary

1. **The existing 12 windows cannot hold rivers.** Each is about 256 m across (volcanic 258 m), so at most 6.6 hectares of land. The biggest catchment in any window is about 1 ha and its mean flow is about 0.5 litres a second (volcanic) or 0.1 L/s (basalt, gneiss, sandstone). Most windows give no channel at all. That is the honest result of running the new hydrology on them (HYDROLOGY_REPORT.md).
2. **What size a river needs** is plain arithmetic: mean flow = rain x runoff x catchment area. At 2000 mm a year and 70 percent runoff, 1 m3/s needs about 22 km2 of catchment (13 km2 at 3000 mm and 80 percent). A burn you could put a footbridge and a small mill on (0.1 m3/s, 2 m wide bankfull) needs about 2.3 km2. So: a "bigger island" for burns and lochs is about 2.5 to 4 km across; for a real salmon river it is about 6 to 12 km, and its shape matters (see 5.1: a ring of hills draining through one gap gives 20 km2 in 7.7 km, a plain cone gives 2.4 km2 in the same size).
3. **The algorithms are cheap and standard**: Priority-Flood pit filling, D8 flow direction, accumulation by one backwards walk, a contributing-area threshold for channels, Strahler order, hydraulic-geometry power laws for width and depth. The whole pass takes 0.1 to 1.3 s in python on 16k to 262k cells here and is a bake step, not a per-frame cost. Runtime only needs a small river graph and two stores per sub-basin.
4. **Water on screen** is ribbons with a two-phase flow-map shader, depth-faded lake planes, vertex-colour wetness, foam at drops: a handful of draw calls. Cost is in the heightfield and the tiling, not in the water.
5. **The new risks** are scale (a 4 to 12 km island is 100 to 2000 times the area of a window: it needs coarse hydrology plus 2 m detail tiles near the player), D8 artefacts (straight 45 degree lines on smooth ground, seen in the invented test), and the existing land tool, which makes small steep rocky islands: a bigger-island generator is a separate piece of work, and the largest one.

## 1. How river networks form on a heightfield, and the cheapest algorithms that look right

### 1.1 The physical picture (what we imitate)
- Rain falls, soaks in (cool wet Atlantic ground is mostly saturated), and runs downhill. Where enough water gathers per metre of contour, flow cuts a channel; below that it sheets across the slope. The area above a channel head shrinks as the ground steepens (about 5 to 45 degrees in soil-mantled hills), so a constant threshold is a simplification. https://www.nature.com/articles/336232a0.pdf (Montgomery and Dietrich 1988, via a search summary; the 1992 paper's equations not read). PLAUSIBLE for the shape. Our threshold: 800 m2 in the window tests, 40,000 m2 on bigger islands (TUNING; real heads run from hundreds to tens of thousands of m2).
- Channels join into a branching tree. Strahler order: a headwater stream is 1, two streams of order n make order n+1 where they meet. Length and area follow Hack's law, length about 1.4 x area^0.6 (km and km2; from memory, PLAUSIBLE): a 22 km2 catchment has a main stem about 9 km long.
- Width, depth and speed follow power laws of discharge: downstream averages about width ~ Q^0.5, depth ~ Q^0.4, velocity ~ Q^0.1 (they must sum to 1 by continuity). https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp252 (Leopold and Maddock 1953; the exponents are the commonly cited averages, the review found per-river spread). VERIFIED as averages. Our constants: bankfull width w = 3.0 Q^0.5 m, depth d = 0.30 Q^0.4 m with Q the bankfull flow, taken as 5 x the mean (TUNING). That gives 2.1 m wide and 0.23 m deep at 0.1 m3/s mean, 6.7 m wide and 0.57 m at 1 m3/s.
- **Meanders**: wavelength about 7 to 12 channel widths (twice the riffle spacing), regression 11 x width; radius of curvature about 2 to 3 widths. https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/0282b/report.pdf , https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70015687 . VERIFIED (search summaries of Leopold and Wolman 1957 and 1960). Meanders need a floodplain and gentle gradient (roughly under 1 percent, PLAUSIBLE); steep burns run straight and step-pool. Sinuosity: 1.0 to 1.1 in glens, 1.2 to 1.5 on a flat floodplain (TUNING). **Braiding** needs steep gradient plus a lot of coarse load and weak banks: on these islands it only belongs on a glacial outwash fan or a short gravel delta, so draw it as a rare special case, not a river type.
- **Valleys and floodplains**: rivers cut a valley, then sit in a flat floor of silt and gravel whose width grows with discharge. Height above the nearest channel (HAND) is the cheap proxy: floor = HAND under about 1 to 2 m. In the code (`hand`).
- **Waterfalls and gorges** occur where the river crosses a hard layer or a step: a knickpoint, with a plunge pool at the base and a gorge as it retreats upstream. The rock map already has hardness: basalt tread and riser, sandstone cap rock, gneiss ledges, volcanic lava-flow fronts. Rule: a channel step of 0.5 m or more at over 19 degrees is a fall candidate (`falls()` in the code; counted per cell, so it is a drop, not a count of falls). TUNING.
- **Deltas and estuaries**: where a river meets slack sea it drops its gravel and sand into a fan or a bar, and the lower valley is flooded by the tide (a ria, drowned valley). The tidal reach is roughly tide range / bed slope (a backwater length scales as depth / slope; from memory, VERIFIED as an approximation): a 2 m range on a 0.1 percent slope reaches about 2 km up; on a 2 percent burn it is a few metres. Steep island burns therefore end in a boulder fan or a plunge onto the shore, not an estuary; estuaries exist only for the rare long gentle river.
- **Springs and seeps**: water comes out where the water table meets the ground: at the foot of a slope, at a rock contact (a hard layer over soft), at a fault, and at the head of every stream. Mark as river heads plus flat wet ground with high wetness index (done as `twi`).
- **Lakes (lochs, tarns)** sit in basins: glacial scours in soft rock, hollows behind a moraine or a lava dam, or a drowned valley. Level = the spill height of the lowest rim; the outlet is the spill point. Priority-Flood gives exactly this (depth = filled - ground). Lake level rises in floods and falls in drought; the outlet river carries the damped signal (see 2.4).
- **Bogs, fens and peat**: blanket bog forms where rain is abundant, drainage poor and evaporation low; the Flow Country (about 1,020 mm over about 200 wet days a year) holds peat deposits over 8 m deep that have built up for 9,000 years (so under 1 mm a year on average). https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/137814/1/map_23_03.pdf (Ratcliffe et al. 2018, via search summary), https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/scotlands-flow-country/ . VERIFIED for amounts; our rule is TUNING: bog where wetness index is high, slope low, ground above about 20 m and not in the splash zone; fen and reed at lake and slow-river edges in water under 1 m; peat as a soil depth of 0.2 to 2 m that the soil rule in `world.py` can read (a note for later, not built).

### 1.2 The algorithms, with cost
All run on a flat cell array, row 0 north, as the exporter stores it. Costs are measured here in python (numpy plus a heapq loop) on one thread; a typed-array JavaScript port is expected to be 10 to 50 times faster (UNVERIFIED, not measured).
| step | what | cheapest form that looks right | measured here |
|---|---|---|---|
| pit filling | no cell may trap water | **Priority-Flood with epsilon** (Barnes, Lehman and Mulla 2014): flood inwards from outlets with a priority queue, every cell gets a parent. Lowest known time complexity for float DEMs. https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.04463 | 16k cells about 0.1 s; 262k cells about 1.3 s (the whole analysis) |
| flow direction | where each cell sends water | the Priority-Flood parent is already a D8 receiver; no second pass | included |
| flow accumulation | area or runoff through each cell | one walk of the pop order backwards (donors before receivers), no sorting | included |
| channels | which cells are rivers | contributing area at least a threshold | included |
| Strahler order | river rank | one more backwards walk with a max-and-count per cell | included |
| lakes | filled depth over a minimum (5 cm, at least 3 cells) | component labelling | included |
| floodplain | HAND | one forward walk: elevation of the first channel cell downstream | included |
| wetness | ln(specific area / slope) | one array expression | included |
| growing a bigger island | stream-power incision (implicit, one pass per step), Braun and Willett 2013; uplift U, K A^m S | offline only | 60 steps on 37k cells about 8 s, 65k cells about 15 s |
Alternatives, and why not: D-infinity or multiple-flow-direction smooths the straight 45 degree lines D8 makes on smooth cones (seen in `hydrology_maps/invented_horseshoe.png`) but costs more and gives braided-looking sheets; at render time the channel is a polyline that we warp with noise (meander wavelength 11 x width), so D8 is fine as the skeleton. Full shallow-water simulation is far beyond a tablet's budget for no visible gain at this scale. For detail, carve the 2 m heightfield from the polyline with a cross-section profile (a distance-to-polyline kernel): the coarse graph decides where, the detail adds banks, bars and meander.

## 2. The cool wet Atlantic island case

### 2.1 Rain by altitude and windward or lee
- Real range in one country: West Highlands around 3,500 mm (Glendessary 3,508 mm), mountain sites over 4 m a year, east coast under 700 mm (Inverbervie 683 mm); the effect is orographic (air forced up cools and rains) and drops off sharply eastward. https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/learning/rain/how-much-does-it-rain-in-the-uk , https://floodforecastingservice.net/2020/12/17/how-do-scotlands-mountains-affect-its-rainfall/ . VERIFIED (Met Office pages, via search summary). In an easterly flow the pattern can reverse.
- Our model: FOOD_AND_WILDLIFE.md uses 900 to 1,400 mm a year for the small flat islands (PLAUSIBLE, TUNING). For a bigger island with hills: base 1,500 mm at the windward coast, plus about 250 mm per 100 m of altitude, plus an upslope term and a lee shadow from the smoothed ground gradient along the wind (`rain_field`), clipped to 0.5 x to 2.2 x base. All TUNING; the checked behaviour is the shape: windward flank wetter than lee (test: more than 1.4 x), plateau wetter than lowland by the altitude term. A 600 m island under westerlies might then run from 1,200 mm on the lee shore to 3,000 mm on the windward summit.
- The macro sea already sends storms from the west on tracks (macro_sea.js); the wind direction at the window is known, so the rain shadow side is a pure function of the wind: no new state.

### 2.2 Runoff, baseflow, floods, droughts
- Runoff coefficient on wet cool upland with thin soil on hard rock is high, about 0.6 to 0.8 of annual rain (PLAUSIBLE; evaporation is low). We use 0.7.
- Hard rock (gneiss, basalt) and peat respond fast: flashy rivers, low baseflow. Permeable sandstone holds more water and flattens the peaks (PLAUSIBLE; per-rock numbers UNVERIFIED). Rule: slow-store share by rock: gneiss and basalt 0.15, sandstone 0.35, peat bog 0.1 slow but very slow to drain after (TUNING).
- Response times scale with catchment size. In the code a quick part is delayed by travel time (hillslope 0.08 m/s, channel 0.8 m/s, TUNING) then smoothed by a linear store, and a slow part drains through a long store. Results: a 1 ha window catchment peaks when the rain stops and halves in about 12 minutes (so window floods are too fast to see); a 19.5 km2 horseshoe catchment under 6 mm/h for 3 h peaks about 5 hours after the storm starts at 6 x its mean flow and takes about 3 hours more to halve (invented terrain; TUNING constants, but the lag-and-tail shape is what real hydrographs have).
- Seasons: wet in autumn and winter (storm season), low flow in late spring and summer. The macro clock starts at day 290 (mid October, macro_sea.js DOY0) and the sky clock gives season indices. Rule: mean-flow multiplier from season (winter 1.4, spring 1.0, summer 0.5, autumn 1.2, TUNING) acting on the baseflow store only; storms act through rainMm.
- Low flow: after a dry spell the slow store empties, small burns shrink to pools, upland springs hold. Fresh water gameplay (FOOD_AND_WILDLIFE.md 1.3) keeps a seep on every island; on a bigger island a stream is the default, with a dry-spell risk.

### 2.3 Snow, frost, ice
At 57.5 degrees north and sea level snow is a few days a year; hills above about 400 to 500 m hold it for weeks in winter (PLAUSIBLE, TUNING). Frost mornings and the first snow dusting are already sky moods (BEAUTIFUL_WEATHER.md section 5). For water: a cold snap freezes pool edges and still shallows of tarns (white rim, thin ice shown by a lake-plane tint and a lower specular), fast burns do not freeze but grow ice lips; snow on the hills adds a delayed melt pulse on the first mild rainy day (rain on snow floods: a gentle but visible spate). All UNVERIFIED in numbers; build as a flag on the existing season and frost state, not as a separate model.

### 2.4 Lakes in time
One scalar per lake: dV/dt = Q_in - Q_out, Q_out = C x width x (level - sill)^1.5 (weir law, C about 1.6; from memory, VERIFIED as the standard broad-crested weir form). Level rises in a flood by a few centimetres to a metre (TUNING), then falls over days: a big lake damps floods downstream. Shore band between summer low and winter high is a place for reeds, mud and a visible "bathtub ring".

### 2.5 Storm surge meeting river flood
macro_sea.js has the inverse barometer (1 cm per hPa, 960 hPa gives 0.53 m) and a tide of about plus or minus 1.1 m in the game. The river mouth level is simply sea level (tide + surge). In the lower valley the river stage is the larger of the river's own normal-depth stage and the sea level; the tidal reach and backwater is about (surge + tide) / bed slope long. Compound flooding (surge and river flood together) is real and is commoner on west-facing coasts than east, up to about 3 to 6 joint events per decade in south-west UK against 0 to 1 on the east, because the same storms bring both. https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/historic-spatial-patterns-of-storm-driven-compound-events-in-uk-e/ , https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/432932/ . VERIFIED (via search summaries). Our islands take storms from the west and have a macro storm clock, so "the storm brings surge and spate together" is the natural default, and the high-tide moment decides how bad (same surge at low tide is harmless).

### 3.1 Life that follows the water (PLAUSIBLE, numbers TUNING)
- **Salmon and sea trout**: autumn spawning in headwaters, mostly October to December (nature.scot gives November to December, wider October to February on big rivers). Fish wait in the main stem and make the last run on a rise in discharge, so the run is gated by spates and by a minimum flow; low autumn flow delays or blocks it. https://www.nature.scot/doc/scotlands-changing-nature-atlantic-salmon , https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/research-prepared-for-parliament/research-briefings/2019/8/19/sb-1948/salmon-migration-and-life-cycle . VERIFIED for timing and flow gating. Barriers: salmon leap falls of about 2 to 3 m (from memory, UNVERIFIED), so a weir without a pass blocks them: a clean link to building.
- **Resident brown trout** need only a pool and cover (any perennial burn above about 0.02 m3/s, TUNING). Eels move at night in autumn rain.
- **Birds**: dipper and grey wagtail on fast rocky burns; heron on shallows; mallard, goosander and swans on lochs; divers on bigger tarns in summer; reed bunting and sedge warbler in reed. **Otter**: holt in bank cover near a pool, fish from burn and shore. **Reed beds**: shallow still water under 1 m, lake margins and backwater.
- **Biomes from moisture and altitude** (extends the existing veg set): riparian woodland (willow, alder, birch, hazel) where HAND < 2 m and sheltered; meadow and rush on the floodplain; blanket bog and wet heath on flat high wet ground; dry heather and bare rock on ridges and exposed summits; salt marsh and gravel fan in the lower tidal reach. Wetness index plus HAND plus altitude plus exposure decides; all in `hydrology.py` except the class mapping.

## 4. Rendering (three.js r128 on tablets; Blender bake)

### 4.1 Page
- **Rivers: ribbon meshes.** Build from the river polyline (smoothed, warped for meanders in the visible tile): a strip with width from the Q law, vertex attributes `along` (distance), `speed`, `turb` (white water 0..1 from slope), `depth`. One merged mesh per 256 m tile, one ShaderMaterial for all. Fragment: two-phase flow map (Vlachos): sample a scrolling normal map twice with half-phase offset and cross-fade, so the restart is hidden; flow direction comes free from the ribbon's UV along-axis, so no flow-map texture is needed on ribbons. https://www.advances.realtimerendering.com/s2010/Vlachos-Waterflow(SIGGRAPH%202010%20Advanced%20RealTime%20Rendering%20Course).pdf , https://graphicsrunner.blogspot.com/2010/08/water-using-flow-maps.html . VERIFIED (technique, via search summary). Colour: shallow = stony bed shows, deep = peat brown or clear green; peat staining brown after rain (tint from the catchment's bog share). Edge: soft alpha edge so it meets the bank without a seam. Cost: about 2 texture reads per pixel, no extra passes.
- **Meanders and banks**: carve the 2 m heightfield with the cross-section so the water ribbon sits 3 to 10 cm under the bank in vertex height; the ribbon is a little lower than the mesh near the edges to avoid z-fighting (polygon offset).
- **Lakes: depth-faded planes.** One flat quad per lake at its level; fragment reads a small height texture of the tile to compute depth = level - ground, alpha and colour from depth (the sea shader already fades with `depth`, see weather.js patch `smoothstep(0,0.6,depth)`: reuse it with lake colours, no waves or tiny ones). Shoreline foam is not needed; a faint rim ring by depth band. Level changes with 2.4 by moving the quad: cheap.
- **Waterfalls**: a vertical ribbon along the drop with streak texture stretched in y and scrolling fast, white mix by `turb`; at the base a foam sprite (the existing wave foam texture) and a plunge-pool disc; mist = at most 40 to 60 soft billboards rising, only when the fall is on screen and quality is not low. Sound is out of scope.
- **Wetness**: per-vertex wetness potential (wetness index, HAND) multiplied by the existing global `wet` uniform (`uWx2.x`, from weather) darkens albedo and adds a little gloss; after rain it lags and drains over hours (a scalar). Zero extra draw calls. Wet rock near falls: a spray mask by distance to falls baked into vertex colour.
- **Wetlands and mist**: bog and fen are vertex colour plus a few reed and bog-cotton instances; standing pools are small lake quads. Valley mist at dawn: a low fog term by height above the valley floor in the existing fog (fogD, `weather.js`), strongest after a cold clear night and over lakes (BEAUTIFUL_WEATHER.md 6.3 mist burning off), no new geometry.
- **Budget (TUNING)**: per visible 256 m tile: 1 ribbon mesh (about 0.5 to 2k triangles for 300 to 600 m of river), 0 to 3 lake quads, 0 to 2 falls; total about 3 to 8 draw calls and under 10k triangles for water. On an older tablet the ribbon shader should drop to one texture read and no edge fade.

### 4.2 Blender bake
The Blender link already has the sea shader with a depth image and a known gap: lochs are dark glossy patches (LANDFORM.md section 7, honest gaps). Bake path: (1) `hydrology.py` writes a river JSON (polyline, width, depth, order) and lake JSON (level, outline) per window; (2) in `landform_scene.py` rivers become curve objects beveled to the width, with the same sea/loch material (depth tint, low roughness, foam from a slope attribute at drops); lakes use `li_scene.make_sea` with a lake depth image; (3) bake wetness and water masks into the terrain vertex colours the way `veg` is baked; (4) export the flow and depth maps as small PNGs (R,G = flow direction x speed, B = depth) for the page so a bake and the live page agree.

## 5. Scale and level of detail

### 5.1 What size gives what (arithmetic; run it with `hydrology.py`)
Mean flow Q = rain x runoff x area. Bankfull taken as 5 x mean.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| trickle (all 12 current windows) | 0.0005 | 0.15 | 0.03 | 0.01 | | 0.09 |
| small burn: trout, ford, footbridge | 0.02 | 0.9 | 0.12 | 0.45 | 0.26 | 0.9 |
| burn with a tiny mill (head 2 m) | 0.1 | 2.1 | 0.23 | 2.3 | 1.3 | 2.3 |

Invented test islands (HYDROLOGY_REPORT.md, stream-power eroded, mean 2000 mm): a radial cone 7.7 km across has 164 river heads, Strahler order 3 and a biggest catchment of 2.4 km2 (0.11 m3/s, 2.2 m wide): a burn island. A horseshoe of hills with one gap, 7.7 km across, gets a 19.5 km2 catchment (0.86 m3/s, 6.2 m wide, order 4); at 10.2 km, 35.9 km2 (1.6 m3/s, 8.5 m, order 5). So the island's shape, not only its size, decides whether a real river exists: valleys that collect (a glacial trough, a breached crater, a tilted plain with one long axis) matter more than extent. A radial cone cannot gather water. TUNING all of it; the arithmetic is VERIFIED, the island shapes are invented.
Honest window answer: for burns of 0.1 m3/s the world window must be about 2.5 to 4 km (a 100 to 200 times larger area than 256 m); for a salmon river about 6 to 12 km and a shape that collects. Our current 512 m region is far too small for either.

### 5.2 Tiles and budgets (tablet)
- Two grids: a **coarse hydrology grid** for the whole island at 8 to 16 m cells (4 km at 8 m = 512 x 512 = 262k cells; 12 km at 32 m = 384 x 384), and **detail tiles** at 2 m (129 x 129 cells = 256 m, 33 kB as int16, the existing export size). A 4 km island at 2 m is 256 tiles, about 8.5 MB as int16 plus masks, so the bake stores all tiles but the page loads only the 3 x 3 around the camera (about 0.3 MB of height plus masks, a few ms to decode); the rest wait on disk or in the file. Detail = coarse height interpolated plus noise plus the carved channel from the polylines, so tiles agree at their seams by construction (both read the same river polyline).
- Hydrology is baked (JS or python, 0.1 to 1.3 s here), not per frame. Runtime state: river graph (a few hundred to a few thousand nodes: position, height, Strahler, area, width), 20 to 60 sub-basins with two stores each, lakes with a level, the rain-factor per sub-basin. Updating all of it each game minute is a few thousand multiply-adds.
- LOD: far tiles draw the river as a thin line mesh of the coarse polyline, no shader scroll; mid tiles ribbons with one texture read; near tiles the full shader and the carved detail. Beyond the island, the sea view shows river mouths as pale brown plumes in the sea colour (a vertex-colour fan at the mouth, scaled by Q), which costs nothing and tells the sailor where a river is.
- Where it fits: tables are 256 to 512 m; a bigger island is explored by moving the window (sailing along the coast and walking). That reuses everything the page does; only the loader (which tiles) and the water layer are new.

## 6. Building on rivers later

### 6.1 Rules (physics first)
| structure | rule | numbers |
|---|---|---|
| ford | a crossing where depth at normal flow is under about 0.4 m and speed under 1 m/s (a person wading); impassable above a threshold flow, not a death trap: you wait or go round | depth from d = 0.30 Q^0.4, stage rises with Q; TUNING |
| footbridge or stone slab | span = bankfull width + 2 x bank; deck above the flood stage with freeboard 0.5 m; stringers and planks use the repair kit parts | width from the Q law |
| weir (low stone or timber dam) | raises the pool by h; backwater reaches about h / slope upstream; floods pass over the crest Q = C b H^1.5 (C about 1.6; from memory, VERIFIED form) | blocks salmon unless it has a pass |
| mill and water wheel | hydraulic power P = rho g Q H x efficiency: 1000 x 9.81 x Q x H x eta. Overshot wheel eta about 0.6 to 0.8, undershot about 0.2 to 0.35 (from memory, PLAUSIBLE). A small corn mill needs on the order of 1 kW (UNVERIFIED), so Q x H of about 0.15 m4/s: Q = 0.1 m3/s with a 2 m head gives about 1.2 kW at eta 0.6 | burn size from 5.1; a lade (leat) that takes a share of Q is a second small channel |
| fish trap | a stone fence or basket in a pool or at a weir, catch rate by run strength and season; a trap in a spate gets washed | uses the run state in 3.1 |
Why the numbers matter: a mill only works on a burn of about 0.1 m3/s or more with a head of 2 m, which is 2 km2 of catchment: it gives a clear reason for a bigger island and for choosing where the mill goes.

### 6.2 Floods and washed-out parts (ties to repair_physics.js and the repair-parts method)
- Flood stage from Q: depth d(Q) by the power law; inundated cells = HAND under the stage (`hand`). Gentle design: the river rises, goes brown, spreads over the floodplain meadow for hours, and withdraws, leaving silt, driftwood, a moved gravel bar. It does not delete buildings.
- Load on a structure: drag F = 0.5 rho Cd A v^2 with v from the Q law (about Q^0.1 at a section, so 1 to 2.5 m/s in floods, TUNING). `repair_physics.js` already has joints with break loads, fatigue, a current that pushes floating parts, buoyancy from a water-height function and a "surge load from the water": the flood supplies the water-height function and the current vector per cell; a lashing that fails frees its plank; planks float down the river and settle in eddies, at bends and at the mouth, where the beach collector finds them (BEACH_COLLECTOR.md). So a flood is a repair job, not a loss: washed-out parts are findable downstream and the player re-fits them. Rule: a flood may loosen parts and move loose ones, but never destroys a part; a wall or a roof beam stays; a ford or footbridge deck above the stage stays.
- Weirs and bridges change floods: a weir raises the stage upstream by its backwater (more spread), a low bridge catches driftwood (a debris-jam flag that raises the stage locally and can be cleared by the player), a mill lade can wash out first. These are small scalar rules, not a flow solver.

## 8. Common mistakes and checklist
Mistakes: rivers that run along ridges or uphill (fill pits first); every valley with the same width (use the Q law); rivers that end in the middle of land (a pit not filled, or a threshold hole: check that every head reaches sea); a river wider than its valley floor; meanders on steep ground; a perfectly straight 45 degree river (D8 artefact); a lake with a tilted surface or no outlet; waterfalls where there is no rock step; estuaries on steep burns; equal rain everywhere on a hilly island; floods at the wrong time (flood at the moment rain starts: real peaks lag the rain by the travel time); salmon above an unpassable fall; bog on a steep slope; a bridge under flood stage; whole settlements deleted by a flood.
Checklist (judge by eye): burns thin at the top, joined, fatter downstream; riffles and pools alternate every 5 to 7 widths in gentle reaches; lochs lie in hollows with a visible outlet; falls on hard layers with a plunge pool and a short gorge; bog flat and pale-brown on high plateaus; reeds only in shallows; wet dark rock beside falls and after rain; river level rises hours after a storm and falls over a day, lake later and slower; brown water after rain, clear in drought; mouth plume in the sea colour; tide shows on the lower reach only on gentle rivers.

## 9. What is solid, what is invented, what is faked
- **Solid (standard, sourced or tested)**: Priority-Flood and the receiver graph; mass is conserved; no uphill flow; Strahler order and area monotone downstream; hydraulic-geometry exponents (0.5, 0.4, 0.1); meander wavelength 7 to 12 widths; the Scottish rainfall range and the orographic cause; salmon timing and flow gating; compound flooding being commoner on west coasts; the flow-map technique. All 61 tests of `test_hydrology.py` pass on 4 worlds (volcanic 4, gneiss 153, sandstone 139, basalt 56) and the rest on all 12.
- **Plausible, not pinned**: runoff 0.6 to 0.8; baseflow shares by rock; Hack's law constants (from memory); tidal reach length; falls leapt by salmon; wheel efficiencies; peat build-up in our model.
- **Invented (TUNING)**: every constant in `hydrograph` (speeds, stores, quick and slow shares); channel thresholds; the rain-by-altitude gain (250 mm per 100 m) and the upslope gain; bankfull = 5 x mean; width and depth coefficients; season multipliers; fall thresholds; mill power need; all costs in days; the two invented island shapes.
- **Faked in the game**: no sediment transport (bars, deltas are rules), no groundwater model beyond one slow store, no 2D flood hydraulics (flood extent is HAND under a stage), no real braiding, meanders are a warp of a D8 skeleton, rain over a bigger island is a uniform rate times a factor map plus a front delay, lakes are one scalar.
- **Could not verify**: Hack's law constants and the Strahler definitions from a page (from memory); exact catchment sizes of small salmon burns; per-rock baseflow indices; tidal-reach lengths for island burns; mill power per millstone; Wikipedia and the original Leopold-Maddock tables (no access in this session).
- **Existing windows**: only the volcanic ones give river-like skeletons (7 to 12 heads, 360 to 545 m of channel, order 2) because the cone is steep and the land fraction is 51 to 65 percent; gneiss, sandstone and basalt windows give 0 to 3 heads of 8 to 33 m and a few pond-sized pools (3 to 32 cells), flows of 0.04 to 0.11 L/s. Use them as seeps, springs and tiny pools, not rivers.
- Method note on lake detection: lakes are pits filled by at least 5 cm and at least 3 cells; the pits in the windows are mostly small hollows of 20 to 390 m2 (ponds, tarns, rock pools).
