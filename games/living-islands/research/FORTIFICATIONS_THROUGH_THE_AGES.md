# Fortifications through the ages: styles you can play as (research note)

Status: research note for Living Islands (branch citylab/cloud-islands). Dated 24 Nov 2026. Follows `RESEARCH_FIRST.md` and the "Fortifications and a head-to-head mode" section of `EVOLUTION_TREE.md`.
Real place and fort names appear only in this note, with sources. Code and data files carry none (`fortify/ballistics_toy.py` uses generic names).
Tone: hopeful. This is architecture and engineering and, at most, a friendly skirmish with crumbling walls and dust. It has no weapon-making steps. Ballistics are game numbers. Robots are relics and take no part.

## 0 How to read this note

Confidence: **High** = stated in a source I read the text of (in practice, a search result summary of a heritage, museum or reference page). **Medium** = standard knowledge, consistent with a source seen, not checked line by line. **Low** = my memory or estimate. **TUNING** = a game number. Honest summary: this session's web fetch tool could not open full pages (the hosts did not resolve, so no full page was read). Everything marked High comes from search-result summaries of pages I could not open. Everything else is Medium or Low. Section 11 lists what I could not verify.

Terms in one line each:
- **Rampart**: the big bank or wall. **Parapet**: the low front lip you shoot over. **Ditch**: the dug trench in front. **Scarp**: the fort side of the ditch. **Counterscarp**: the far side of the ditch.
- **Glacis**: the long gentle slope outside, built so attackers have nowhere to hide. **Covered way**: a path on top of the counterscarp behind its own parapet.
- **Bastion**: a pointed block that sticks out of the wall. **Curtain**: the straight wall between bastions or towers. **Ravelin**: a small triangular island in front of a curtain.
- **Casemate**: a gun room with walls and a roof. **Barbette**: a gun on top, firing over a parapet. **Embrasure**: the opening a gun fires through.
- **Revetment**: facing that holds a bank steep (timber, stone, brick, gabions).

## 2 The styles, one by one

Each style has the same eight parts: what it is, why it worked, what beat it, how it is built, typical sizes, how defenders fought, what it looks like, how it plays.

### 2.1 Earth and timber: ditch, bank, palisade, motte

- **What it is.** Dig a ditch. Throw the soil into a bank on the inside. Put a timber fence (palisade) on top. In the "motte and bailey" form a steep mound (the motte) carries a timber tower. A yard (the bailey) sits beside it, with its own bank and fence.
- **Why it worked.** It uses only what is on site: soil, trees, labour. It can go up in days to weeks. A ditch and a steep bank slow people on foot. A fence stops a quick rush. (Medium)
- **What beat it.** Fire (timber burns). Rain slumps banks. A large force can fill a ditch. A siege starves the defenders. It was replaced by stone where stone and time allowed. (Medium)
- **How it is built.** A V or flat-bottomed ditch. The dug soil piled inside, often on a base of turf and timber. Posts set in a trench, side by side, with a walkway behind. A gate of timber posts with a tower over it. The bank face may be cut steep and held with turf or timber (revetment). (Medium)
- **Typical sizes (Low to Medium).** Ditch 2 to 4 m wide and 1.5 to 3 m deep. Bank 2 to 4 m high. Palisade posts 2.5 to 4 m tall above the walk. Motte 5 to 10 m high and 30 to 60 m across at the base (Low). Walls are thin: a palisade is 0.2 to 0.4 m of timber (Low).
- **How defenders fought.** From the walkway behind the fence, and from the tower on the motte. They threw and shot down the slope. The ditch and the steep bank were the real defence. The fence held up a rush for a while.
- **What it looks like (for drawing).** Silhouette: a low ragged line of pointed stakes on a green bank with a dark ditch below. A squat timber tower on a green cone. Palette: fresh turf green, raw soil brown, silver-grey weathered posts, a few orange-brown patches of rust-coloured rot, smoke grey. Rope lashings and a few lashed crossbars.
- **Game style: "Earth and Timber".** Strengths: very cheap, quick, repairable with planks. Earth takes flat shot well. Weaknesses: timber fails fast to splash and fire. Banks slump in rain and under splash. Needs constant repair. Fits the shelter ladder at rungs 1 and 2 (driftwood and turf). See section 8.

### 2.2 Hill forts

- **What it is.** An enclosure on a hilltop, ringed with one or more banks and ditches (multivallate = many banks). Entrances are narrow, often with long inturned passages. They were common in the British Iron Age and in other parts of Europe. (High for the general description: Wessex Hillforts Project and university excavation reports in search results.)
- **Why it worked.** The hill did half the work. Attackers climbed in the open, on a slope, under missiles. Several banks in a row meant a defender could fall back. (Medium)
- **What beat it.** A long siege. A large, organised army with engineering (the Roman army took many in Britain). Fire where the rampart held timber. (Medium)
- **How it is built.** Dug ditch, with the spoil thrown on the bank's outer face to make a steep loose slope that attackers scramble up to reach a timber palisade at the top. A "box rampart" is a double row of posts about 3 m apart, tied together with cross timbers, then filled with earth and rubble. Some had timber-laced stone fronts. Later forts may have a rock-cut ditch. (High: Cardiff and Herefordshire excavation reports in search results.)
- **Typical sizes.** Enclosed area from a fraction of a hectare to tens of hectares (Medium). Ramparts commonly 2 to 6 m high (Low). One popular article gives 14 m for a very large bank (I could not check it: Low). Ditch widths I could not source.
- **How defenders fought.** From the crest behind the palisade, with throwing stones and darts, and by holding the gate passage, which forced attackers to turn and show their side. (Medium)
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: concentric green rings on a hill like a stack of lips, a dark notch for the gate, a few round huts inside, timber palisade on the crest. Palette: grass green, chalk white or sandy soil scars on the ditch face, dark thatch, silver timber.
- **Game style: "Hill Fort".** Strength: terrain does the work. The more of the slope you use, the cheaper the defence. Weakness: big perimeter needs many parts, and a defender cannot cover all of it. Fits the world well: the landform windows have real cones and ridges.

### 2.3 Early stone: walls, gatehouses, towers

- **What it is.** A stone wall with towers and a gatehouse. Examples: town walls, Roman forts and walls, early castles. (Medium)
- **Why it worked.** Stone does not burn and a tall wall stops a rush. Towers stick out so defenders can shoot along the wall face. A gatehouse concentrates the weak point (the door) into a strong block. (Medium)
- **What beat it.** Rams and picks at the base. Undermining (digging under the foot and propping, then burning the props, so the wall falls). Siege towers and ladders. Starvation. (Medium)
- **How it is built.** Two faces of cut or rough stone with a rubble and mortar core (a "double skin"). Lime mortar. A plinth or batter (a wider foot) at the base. Wall walks with a parapet and gaps (crenellations). Gate with a portcullis (a sliding grid) and double doors. (Medium)
- **Typical sizes.** Wall 1.5 to 3 m thick and 6 to 10 m high (Low). Towers 8 to 12 m across, 12 to 15 m high (Low). Gate passage 3 to 4 m wide.
- **How defenders fought.** From the wall walk, from tower windows and loops (slits), and from above the gate. Dropping things on people at the foot was the standard answer to rams and ladders.
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: a straight wall with square teeth, square or round towers at the corners, a gate block bulging forward. Palette: pale grey and warm sand stone, dark slits, orange or grey tiled roofs, lichen on the north face.
- **Game style: "Stone Wall".** Strengths: hits are absorbed in many small parts, and it does not burn. Weaknesses: tall, so a flat shot hits a big target and a lob drops on top; stone falls in chunks that hurt what is behind. Unlocks at rung 2 (drystone) and gets better at rung 4 (lime mortar).

### 2.4 Medieval castles: keep, curtain, machicolation, concentric design, siege engines

- **What it is.** A fortified home, often on a rock or a mound, with a strong tower (the keep), walls (the curtain), towers on the corners, and a gatehouse. The "concentric" design (13th century) puts a low outer wall round a higher inner wall, so defenders on the inner wall can shoot over the outer one. (High: concentric castle and Beaumaris and Caerphilly pages in search results.)
- **Why it worked.** Height, thickness, and layers. An attacker who breaks one layer finds another. Machicolations are galleries sticking out of the top of a wall, with openings in the floor. They let defenders drop things on the foot of the wall without leaning out. Murder holes in a gate passage roof do the same (Caerphilly's main gatehouse has two rows: High). Earlier timber versions are called hoardings (Medium).
- **What beat it.** Mining (digging under a tower so it drops). Siege engines. Long sieges. Later, cannon. The tall thin tower was the first thing guns killed. (Medium)
- **How it is built.** Stone curtain on a plinth, towers (round towers are harder to undermine at a corner than square ones: Medium), a moat or ditch, a drawbridge, a gatehouse with a portcullis and murder holes, a keep as the last refuge, a well inside. The concentric layout of Beaumaris: inner wall about 4.7 m thick and 11 m high, with the gap between the two walls about 18 m (High: one source, search result; Medium on the numbers).
- **Siege engines at game level only.** A counterweight thrower (trebuchet) is a big arm with a heavy box at one end and a sling at the other. Sources disagree on performance: stones from tens of kilograms to over a hundred, ranges from about 100 m to about 275 m for the largest (High that the sources conflict: USC Illumin, PBS NOVA, the Scientific American article by Chevedden and colleagues in search results). I give no building details. It is slow (minutes per shot), needs a crew, and hits walls from above and in front. A game "thrower" should lob, splash, hit slowly, and be easy to out-range with a gun.
- **How defenders fought.** From the wall top and gate; sallying out to burn engines; countermining; holding the inner ward. (Medium)
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: a stepped skyline, outer wall low and inner wall tall, round towers with conical or flat roofs, a heavy gatehouse with two towers, a keep rising from the middle. Palette: grey stone with warm orange torchlight, dark slate or timber roofs, pale limewash patches, moss at the foot.
- **Game style: "Castle".** Strengths: height gives sight and range; layers give time. Weakness: a lob or a heavy gun that hits the tallest tower topples the rest; the keep is a fixed target. Plays as a slow defence: strong in a siege, poor against fast flat guns.

### 2.5 The gunpowder revolution: thick low walls, artillery towers, gun ports

- **What it is.** After heavy guns arrived (14th and 15th centuries) tall thin medieval walls failed. Engineers made fortifications low, thick, round and gun-armed. The Tudor "device" forts in England are a good example: a low circular central tower with low semicircular bastions round it, gun platforms on several levels and gun ports. (High: English Heritage Deal Castle and Kent heritage record in search results.)
- **Why it worked.** A low profile is a small target. A thick wall (and a thick earth fill behind) absorbs the blow. A round shape has no flat face to meet a shot square-on (the "deflects round shot" claim is common but I found it only on one marker: Low). Guns on the fort could fire in all directions. (Medium)
- **What beat it.** Better and bigger guns, then the angled design that gave better flanking cover (section 2.6). The round forts also left dead ground at the foot of the wall. (Medium)
- **How it is built.** Thick stone or brick walls with a packed earth or rubble core, rounded parapets, gun ports (embrasures) at several levels, a moat. Smoke inside the gun rooms was a problem: flues were added (Low).
- **Typical sizes.** Walls several metres thick (Low on exact numbers, Medium on "thick"). Height a few storeys at most. The aim was a "squat" shape.
- **How defenders fought.** By firing from gun ports on all levels at ships or at an attacking army, and by sheer mass: a defender could keep firing while the wall took hits.
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: a low clover or flower shape from above, rounded parapets like biscuit tops, a short thick central tower. Palette: pale stone, brown timber doors, black gun port holes, grey slate.
- **Game style: "Squat Fort".** Strengths: tough against early flat shot; guns cover all sides. Weaknesses: fixed arcs of fire, dead ground at the foot, vulnerable to a lob on its flat roof. A good bridge style between castle and bastion.

### 2.6 Bastion and star forts

- **What it is.** A fort whose outline is a polygon with pointed projections (bastions) at the corners. In front: a wide ditch, a covered way, and a long low slope (the glacis). Often with small triangular outworks (ravelins) in front of the straight walls. Developed in Italy from about 1500 and spread across Europe. In France, Vauban's forts and many town defences (a World Heritage group) are the most famous. (High for the parts and what they do: Réseau Vauban glossary, battlefields.org glossary, Ordnance Society guide in search results.)
- **Why angles matter.** Round towers leave blind spots (dead ground) next to the wall. A pointed bastion has flat faces and flanks, so guns on one bastion can fire along the face of the next and cover every point in front of the wall. That removes dead zones (High: multiple sources). The flat faces also meet shot at an angle, so many shots glance off (Medium). Low, thick, earth-backed walls take shot well (Medium).
- **The parts.**
  - Glacis: a gently sloped bank joining the top of the covered way to the ground in front. Its height and angle were chosen to hide the fort's wall from direct fire and still let defenders fire over it (High: Réseau Vauban glossary).
  - Covered way: the path on top of the counterscarp behind its own parapet. Defenders can move out of sight and fire across the glacis (High).
  - Counterscarp: the outer side of the ditch. Scarp: the fort's side of the ditch, often a masonry wall (High).
  - Berm: a ledge between the scarp wall and the bank that keeps stone falling from filling the ditch (High).
  - Ravelin: a V-shaped outwork in front of a curtain, covering the front between two bastions (High).
  - Traverse: a thick mound across a path or the covered way to stop fire running along it (High).
- **What beat it.** Time and engineering. The attacker dug zigzag trenches toward the fort (parallels and approaches), set up batteries, breached the wall at the foot of a bastion, then stormed. A good fort made the siege long enough for a relief army to arrive. Later, rifled guns made masonry walls fail (section 2.8). (Medium)
- **How it is built.** Earth rampart, 10 to 25 m thick at the base (Low), faced with a stone or brick scarp wall a few metres thick (Low), a wide ditch (10 to 30 m: Low), a covered way, a glacis sloping gently out (about 1 in 10 to 1 in 20: Low). Brick or stone faces, earth behind: shot buries itself in the earth.
- **How defenders fought.** Guns on the bastion faces and flanks, small arms from the covered way, sorties to wreck the attackers' trenches, and repair of the breach every night (Medium).
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: from above, a star or a sharp-cornered polygon, from the side a long low green line with a brick or stone face at the foot and a grassy slope in front. Palette: warm brick red or grey stone at the foot, bright turf on top, dark ditch, small sentry boxes at the bastion tips.
- **Game style: "Star Fort".** The best "clever shape" style. Strengths: no dead ground, every part covers its neighbours, a lean-back face is hit at a shallower angle by flat fire. Weaknesses: many parts to build and repair, and it takes room (not good on a tiny island). A lob over the glacis drops on the roof, and a slope that deflects a flat shot takes a plunging shell nearly square-on (the toy tests this).

### 2.7 Coastal batteries and towers (18th and 19th centuries)

- **What it is.** Defences on the shore against ships and landings. Open **barbette** batteries (guns on a platform firing over a parapet, low earth or masonry). **Casemate** batteries (guns inside vaulted rooms with embrasures). **Round towers** with a gun on the roof (Martello towers, from about 1805, Britain and elsewhere). **Signal stations** and beacons (fire or flag) to pass warning along a coast. (High for towers: Britannica, English Heritage and Kent archaeology pages in search results.)
- **Why it worked.** A gun on land is steady and can be big. A tower on a headland sees far. A line of towers gives overlapping fire. A shore battery on a cliff has a plunging angle that ships cannot match, and a wave-tossed ship cannot aim well. (Medium)
- **What beat it.** Ships' heavy broadsides at close range. Then rifled guns, shells and armour (section 2.8). Open barbette guns were exposed to shell. Towers were too small a platform for bigger guns. (Medium)
- **How it is built.** Martello-type tower: round, two floors and a flat roof, one entrance at first-floor height by a ladder, a stair inside the thick wall, a single heavy gun on a swivel on the roof behind a parapet, a store and a cistern inside. Walls are thicker on the seaward side. Brick or stone, earth or lead on the roof. (High: Britannica and Kent archaeology in search results.)
- **Typical sizes.** Tower up to about 12 m high. Walls: sources differ: 3 m (9 feet) thick on the seaward side (Britannica), 4 m at the base to 1.8 m at the parapet (a Martello society document), up to 4 m (a Suffolk source). Parapet about 1.8 m (6 feet). Garrison one officer and 15 to 25 men. One 24-pounder gun on the roof (High on all of these as reported; the thickness varies by place and by where it is measured).
- **How defenders fought.** From the roof against landing boats and ships, with a pivoting gun. In a battery, from low platforms with the parapet behind the gun. By signal: a lit fire or a flag told the next station.
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: a short round tower that tapers toward the top like a squat bottle with a flat top, a rope or ladder at a high door, a low earth battery with dark gun muzzles through gaps in the parapet. Palette: weathered yellow-grey brick or grey stone, black iron, sea-green copper, white gull stain, green turf on batteries.
- **Island and coast extras.** Harbour chains and booms (a heavy chain or a log boom across a harbour mouth) stop boats. Boat obstacles (stakes, wrecks, sunk stone) at the landing beach. Signal fires on headlands. A lighthouse is a lookout by day and a fire at night. Tide and surf are a defence: a landing on a rocky coast waits on the tide, and breakers hide rocks. (Medium)
- **Game style: "Coastal Battery".** Strengths: sea range, easy to build with turf and stone, good against boat raids and storms-at-sea modes. Weaknesses: fixed arcs, little cover from above, easily outflanked from the land side. Ties directly to the existing lighthouse and signal-fire ladder in EVOLUTION_TREE.md.

### 2.8 19th century masonry forts, early iron and armour

- **What it is.** Big masonry forts (polygonal and sea forts) with several tiers of guns. Then forts with iron shields, iron-faced embrasures, rotating iron turrets, and granite faces. (Medium)
- **Why it worked, and what beat it.** Tall granite or brick walls with many guns were hard to beat with smoothbore shot. Rifled guns changed that. The case people quote is Fort Pulaski in 1862: its brick walls were about 7.5 feet (2.3 m) thick, on a site where smoothbore guns were thought useless beyond about 700 yards (about 640 m). Rifled guns on a nearby island breached a corner in about 30 hours and the fort surrendered. (High: National Park Service pages in search results. One page gives wall thickness that went from 8 feet to 2 to 4 feet; the sources disagree on whether that was the wall or the moat, so treat the wall figure as uncertain.) After that, masonry forts were seen as out of date.
- **How it is built.** Brick or granite casemates in tiers, with iron plate on gun fronts, iron doors, earth piled in front of the walls, and later low sunken forts with turrets. Sea forts out in a roadstead stand on artificial islands. (Medium)
- **Typical sizes.** Walls 1.5 to 3 m of masonry; iron plates a few inches to over a foot on the best (Low).
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: a long rows-of-arches face (the tiers of gun rooms), black iron shutters on the arches, granite blocks, a flat top with turrets. Palette: grey granite, red brick, black iron, rust streaks, green weed at the waterline.
- **Game style: "Iron Fort".** Strengths: strong face, many guns. Weakness: a lot of metal and masonry that an island cannot make (it needs trade and relearned skills). A showpiece style for "later". Not a starter.

### 2.9 World War One trenches and dugouts

- **What it is.** A system of ditches in the ground, in several lines, with wire in front. (High for fire step, traverses, duckboards, dugouts and reverse-slope siting: Long Long Trail, Canadian Encyclopedia and Spartacus Educational pages in search results.)
- **Why it worked.** Modern rifles and machine guns and shells made standing in the open deadly, and the way to survive was to dig. A trench cuts the target to almost nothing.
- **What beat it.** Heavy shell (a long bombardment), gas, tanks, and new tactics (small groups infiltrating, creeping barrages). Over time defence was made deeper, with several lines and strong points rather than one line. (Medium)
- **How it is built.**
  - A fire step: a raised ledge (about 2 to 3 feet, so about 0.6 to 0.9 m high, High) to see and shoot over the front lip.
  - Traverses: the line zigzags in bays so a shell or a raider cannot sweep the whole length (High). Bay length 10 to 15 yards in one source (Low reliability).
  - Duckboards: slatted wood on the floor against mud (High).
  - Dugouts: rooms cut into the rear wall of the trench (High). Deeper ones were cut well below ground (Medium, depth Low).
  - Barbed wire belts in front (Medium).
  - Reverse-slope siting: engineers preferred a position just behind a ridge, hidden from direct observation and from flat-trajectory fire (High).
  - Depth: lines laid out in a zone over a kilometre or more deep (the Canadian Encyclopedia figure of 1.5 km is for the whole system: High).
- **Typical sizes.** Firing trench about 2 m deep (Medium; I could not source a standard), about 1 m wide at the bottom. Dugouts 2 to 3 m below the floor, much deeper for a few (Low).
- **How defenders fought.** From the fire step, from machine-gun posts in the bays, and with a "listening post" out in front. By staying hidden and shooting when attackers crossed the open ground (called "no man's land").
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: a ground-level zigzag cut in brown mud with a slightly raised lip of sandbags, wire stakes in rows, a few timber door frames of dugouts. Palette: mud brown, grey wet clay, silver-grey timber, rust-brown wire, sandbag tan, puddles.
- **Game style: "Trench Line".** Strengths: cheap, digs into any hill, tough against everything flat, and in the toy a reverse-slope trench cannot be hit by flat fire at all. Weaknesses: needs a drainage and repair loop (wet ground, slumping, flooding with tide on an island), and a lob can drop in. A good player style: the best use is a trench behind a ridge.

### 2.10 World War Two concrete

- **What it is.** Reinforced concrete forts. Pillboxes (small gun houses). Casemates (bigger gun rooms). Coastal gun batteries with heavy guns in concrete gun houses. Anti-tank obstacles (concrete teeth, steel hedgehogs, ditches). Camouflage. The "Atlantic Wall" along the western European coast is the largest example, and the Maginot Line in France the best-known linear fortress system. (High for both: Atlantic Wall and Maginot pages in search results.)
- **Why it worked.** A concrete shell a few metres thick can stand direct hits from field guns. Gun slits narrow to a small target. Covered inside, a crew can hold a crossing point. A line of mutually covering positions turns a beach into a killing ground. (Medium)
- **What beat it.** Very heavy shells and bombs, naval bombardment, going round (the Maginot Line was bypassed through a sector without strong works, in the Ardennes: High). Isolated bunkers can be taken from behind, from above, or by cutting off supply. Camouflage and surprise also decided the outcome. (Medium)
- **How it is built.** Standard designs were used by the thousand ("Regelbau" = standard build). Thickness classes were set: class A with walls and roof about 3.5 m thick, class B about 2 m, and one-off special works with concrete up to 5 m (High: Atlantic Wall Wadden Sea site and Regelbau page in search results). Floors might be covered to soften shock from a hit (High). Steel rebar inside, a stepped or "bent" entrance so blast does not run in, an armoured door, a ventilation shaft, and fire slits facing along the beach rather than out to sea so ships cannot see them (Medium). The Maginot Line forts: tunnels and rail lines several to up to 30 m below the surface, and over 1.5 million cubic metres of concrete (High: search results), with gun blocks and steel cloches (domes) that rise from concrete wells.
- **Typical sizes.** Pillbox 3 to 6 m across, roof 0.5 to 2 m thick (Medium). Casemate 8 to 12 m across, walls 2 to 3.5 m (High for the thickness classes). Concrete teeth 1 to 1.5 m high (Medium).
- **How defenders fought.** From behind slits, with machine guns in flanking fire along the wall or beach. Mines and obstacles held attackers in the open. Heavier guns in casemates shot at ships. Camouflage netting, paint, soil on the roof, dummy buildings and fake positions hid and confused.
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: low flat-roofed grey blocks half sunk into a dune or hill, one thin black slit, a stepped side wall. Palette: raw cement grey, rust streaks from rebar, dark slit, sand-and-grass camouflage paint, torn netting tan.
- **Game style: "Concrete".** Strengths: very strong per part, hard to hit, good against most arcs. Weaknesses: expensive (needs cement, steel and know-how that an island must trade or relearn), cracks and spalls when overloaded (and a spall flies inside), fixed position, and a heavy gun or a steep arc beats it. A late-ladder style.

### 2.11 Cold War and modern

- **What it is.** Deep command bunkers, hardened aircraft shelters, missile silos, tunnels in mountains, dispersal (spreading things out), decoys, radar and signal sites. (Medium)
- **Why static forts faded.** A fixed target that a precise weapon can find is a target, however thick. Mobile units, air power and guided weapons made a fixed line less useful than depth, dispersal and speed. Fixed forts remain useful where they are hard to find, deep, or only part of a mobile defence. (Medium)
- **Drones, briefly.** Small flying scouts and strike drones make open ground and hidden positions harder to rely on. They favour cover, nets, tunnels, decoys and spread-out positions. They are a modern threat only and I recommend leaving them out of the game, or limiting them to a "weather and spotting" story (see open questions). (Low)
- **How it is built.** Deep tunnels, thick concrete, blast doors, filtered air, power and water inside. (Medium)
- **What it looks like.** Silhouette: almost nothing above ground: a mound, a vent, a door set into a hill, a radio mast. Palette: grass-covered earth, grey concrete, orange rust on steel doors, dark shadows.
- **Game style: "Deep Bunker".** Strength: nearly immune to a flat or lob shot when buried deep. Weakness: it does nothing on its own. It is not a good head-to-head style because it ends the game by being unbeatable. Best as a quiet builder or visitor mode, or as a late-era "hide" strategy that scores for survival, not for firing.

## 3 Hill-specific features (all eras)

- **Use the slope.** Build on the crest and the shoulders. A defender above a slope shoots down and sees far. An attacker uphill is slowed and in the open. (Medium)
- **Reverse slope.** A position just behind the crest cannot be seen or hit by flat fire from the front. The attacker must lob or climb to the crest. The toy model tests exactly this: no flat shot reaches a bunker behind a 14 m ridge, but a steep arc does. The cost: a short view, so the defender must put a lookout on the crest (a post, a signal fire). (High for the idea: Long Long Trail in search results.)
- **Terraces.** Stepped flat strips cut into a slope give level gun and wall places, and each terrace edge is a mini-rampart. Easy to draw as stepped rings. (Medium)
- **Tunnels.** Dug into rock or firm earth: a hidden route between positions, a store, a shelter from shell. Needs props or lining in soft ground. Costs digging and time. (Medium)
- **Cut and bank.** Dig into the slope and throw the soil in front: you get a ditch and a bank at once, and the soil needs no carrying. (Medium)
- **Ditch across a neck.** A "promontory fort" cuts the neck of a headland with one bank and ditch, and the sea guards the rest. Perfect for small islands. (Medium)
- **Watch the water.** Cut ground must drain. Water and the island's tide logic must stay honest (see EVOLUTION_TREE.md, digging). Low trenches near the shore flood at high tide. (Medium)

## 4 Island and coast features

- **Shore batteries.** Low earth and stone batteries on points, covering the approach and the landing beach.
- **Harbour chains and booms.** A chain or log boom across a harbour mouth. It stops small boats and slows big ones. Game: a single "boom" part with hit points and a gap that can be opened by the owner. (Medium)
- **Boat obstacles.** Rows of stakes and sunk stone below the high-water mark, hidden at high tide and exposed at low. (Medium)
- **Signal fires.** A fire on a headland, then a lamp on a post, then a lighthouse. Already on the signal ladder in EVOLUTION_TREE.md. Each step reaches farther and shines through thicker fog. In a fort game the signal is the early warning. (High: existing note.)
- **Lighthouses as lookouts.** The tower is a perfect lookout by day. A lens needs glass (rung 4). (Medium)
- **Tide and surf as defence.** On a rocky island the landing place is at one or two spots, and only at the right tide. Surf on a beach breaks small boats. A good player builds where the sea already does the work. (Medium)
- **Storm as an enemy.** Waves and wind damage a battery too. This links to the existing weather states.

## 5 How offence and defence leapfrog

Each new attack beat the old defence, and each new defence answered it. The table shows how each defence style fares against each attack type. Scores are game-level guesses (TUNING): "strong" means it usually holds, "ok" means it holds with repair, "weak" means it fails fast. The right-hand column says what answered it.

| Defence | Rush on foot | Fire | Lob / thrower | Smoothbore gun | Rifled / heavy gun | Steep shell (mortar) | Sea landing | Next defence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Earth and timber | ok | weak | weak | ok (earth strong, timber weak) | weak | weak | ok | stone |
| Hill fort | strong | ok | ok | ok | weak | weak | n/a | stone, then guns |
| Early stone | strong | strong | weak | weak | weak | weak | strong | thicker, rounder, lower |
| Castle | strong | strong | ok (layers) | weak (tall tower) | weak | weak | strong | squat fort |
| Squat fort | strong | strong | ok | ok | weak | ok | strong | bastion |
| Bastion fort | strong | strong | strong (earth soaks it) | strong | ok to weak (masonry face) | ok | strong | rifle-proof: concrete, depth |
| Coastal battery or tower | ok | strong | ok | ok | weak | weak | strong | casemate, iron, concrete |
| 19th c. masonry and iron | strong | strong | strong | strong | weak (Pulaski) | ok | strong | concrete |
| WWI trench | ok | n/a | ok (needs deep dugouts) | strong (low target) | ok | weak | n/a | deeper, many lines, concrete |
| WWII concrete | strong | strong | strong | strong | ok (heavy only) | strong at 2 m, weaker at 1 m | strong | going round, air, depth |
| Deep bunker | strong | strong | strong | strong | strong | strong | n/a | precision weapons, dispersal |

How to read the arms race in one line each:
1. Walls got taller, so engines (and mining) arrived. (Medium)
2. Guns arrived, so walls got low and thick. (High: Tudor forts pages)
3. A round, low fort left dead ground, so bastions made pointed shapes and flanks. (High)
4. Rifled shell outranged and cracked masonry, so forts went to earth, concrete and depth. (High for the Pulaski turning point; Medium for the rest)
5. Concrete held against guns, so the answer was to go round, go above, or hit from afar. (Medium)
6. Precision and mobility made fixed lines less useful, so depth, decoys and dispersal. (Medium)

## 6 Game mechanics (all numbers are TUNING)

The model in `fortify/ballistics_toy.py` runs these numbers and the tests in `fortify/test_ballistics_toy.py` check them. See `fortify/FORTIFY_MODEL.md` for the report.

### 6.1 Weapon ladder (matched to eras)

Range is the best-angle range in the toy's units (metres in a 258 m window). Arc: "flat" fires nearly straight, "lob" is a high arc. Damage is hit points before the material factor and the angle factor.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Scavenged | Hand-thrown stone, sling (not in the toy) | 30 to 60 | flat to lob | 2 | 0 | 1 to 2 | people, not walls | everything |
| 3 Castle | Lobber (counterweight thrower), toy "thrower" | 111 | lob only (25 to 70 degrees) | 7 | 3.2 | 13 (splash 10) | timber, earth, tall walls | concrete, low walls |
| 5 Gunpowder | Smooth gun, toy "cannon" | 359 | flat (and some lob) | 5.5 | 1.0 | 10 | timber, stone | earth, sloped faces |

### 6.2 Damage by material (factor against each weapon kind)

| Material | Hit points per part per metre thickness | vs lob | vs flat shot | vs steep shell | Splash factor | Cost per m3 (units) | Sensible max thickness (m) | How a part fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Timber | 24 | 1.2 | 1.3 | 1.0 | 1.2 | 0.9 | 1.2 | planks splinter |
| Earth | 30 | 1.3 | 0.55 | 1.0 | 1.4 | 0.28 | 3.5 | earth slumps |
| Stone | 52 | 1.1 | 1.1 | 0.9 | 0.8 | 0.95 | 2.5 | stones fall |
| Concrete | 85 | 0.6 | 0.9 | 0.45 | 0.5 | 2.3 | 3.0 | concrete cracks and spalls |

From the tests (flat fire from the toy "cannon", one part): hits to fail at 0.4, 0.8, 1.6 m thick are timber 1, 2, 3; earth 3, 5, 9; stone 2, 4, 8; concrete 4, 8, 16. Lean-back faces at 0, 30 and 60 degrees, 2.4 m thick, flat fire: stone 12, 13, 21; earth 14, 15, 25; concrete 23, 26, 42.

### 6.3 Wall hit points and angle

- Part hit points = per-metre value x thickness. A part is intact, damaged (at half), or failed.
- Angle: damage x cosine of the angle between the shot and the face normal. Past 72 degrees it glances off for 12 percent.
- A leaning face (30 to 60 degrees) tilts the normal up. It helps against flat fire. A plunging shell arrives nearly square-on to a leaning face, so it does more damage. The shape is therefore a real choice. (Tested.)
- Terrain does the same: a steep front slope deflects, and a hill face absorbs.

### 6.4 How parts fail (for drawing and repair)

| Material | Looks when damaged | Looks when failed | Left behind | Repair (the parts method) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Timber | splinters, cracked posts, leaning | broken planks on the ground | a low stump line | put a new plank or post on its socket; nail or lash |
| Earth | cracks along the top, small slides | slump, a low mound | a low mound (35 percent height) | dig and pack new earth: a work job (person-days), not a loose part |
| Stone | missing corner stones, a crack | a heap of stones | rubble (25 percent height) | pick stones off the heap and set them back (loose parts you can lift: ties to `repair_physics.js`) |
| Concrete | surface flaking (spall), a crack | cracked slab, bent rods | rubble (20 percent height) | very costly to fix: patch only; or replace the whole piece |

The same idea as the wrecked jetty in `REPAIR_PILOT.md`: each part is a body, each place it sits is a socket, and a failed part is something you can lift and put back.

### 6.5 Line of sight and slope

- A shot or a view needs a clear ray over the terrain heightfield, with an eye height at each end (tested: blocked across a 25 m ridge, open from a 30 m tower).
- High ground gives sight and range. The reverse slope hides from flat shot but not from a lob.
- Lookouts, lighthouses and signal fires add sight at distance. At night, fog or rain, the view range falls. The game can reuse the existing light, fog and weather systems: `weather.js` states (fog, rain, storm) and `research/LIGHT_FOG_AND_LIGHTHOUSE.md` (a strong lens light is still seen about 900 m through 200 m fog; a small fire about 550 m). A simple rule: aim error is divided by a visibility number between 0.2 and 1. The toy has this as the `visibility` argument to `duel` (not yet tested).

### 6.6 Wind, fog and night

- Wind is an air velocity from the existing wind. A 6 m/s crosswind moves a 45 degree lob 7.4 m sideways in the toy. The player sees a flag or smoke as a hint, not a number.
- Fog hides the other side's walls, so a player cannot aim, but a lit fire or lamp shows up. Night makes a lit fort visible and a dark fort hidden. Camouflage matters (a grassy roof, dark paint).
- Rain makes earth slump faster and trenches flood. Storm closes the sea approach.

### 6.7 Digging and what it costs

- Dig a ditch, bank, trench, terrace or a tunnel as a terrain edit on the landform heightfield.
- Labour guess (Low): about 1 to 2 person-days per cubic metre in soft soil; more in rock; much more for a lined tunnel. A 14 m wall with a 2 m ditch beside it is of the order of 100 to 150 m3 (so about 100 to 300 person-days), which is why a shared pool of workers matters.
- Rules that keep the water honest: a cut below sea level floods at the matching tide; a cut that blocks a stream makes a pool; a bank on a slope drains or breaks. The terrain edit must keep the water logic correct.
- Digging into a hill gives a free bank (the spoil) and a hidden position. That is why hill digging is the best-value work in the game.

### 6.8 Build time and cost on the shelter ladder

The fort ladder follows `research/FIRST_SHELTER_AND_MATERIALS.md`: rung 1 driftwood and cloth, rung 2 turf and stone, rung 3 sawn timber and rope, rung 4 lime and glass. A fort rung needs the same material at least.

| Fort rung | Style you can build | Materials | Needs from outside | Labour (person-days, a 14 m wall with a ditch; TUNING, Low) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 (shelter rung 1 to 2) | Earth and Timber: ditch, bank, driftwood stakes | scavenged spars, turf, soil | nothing | 20 to 60 |
| F2 (rung 2 to 3) | Hill Fort and Stone Wall: dry stone on a bank | stone, turf, some cut timber | nothing | 80 to 250 |
| F3 (rung 3 to 4) | Castle and Squat Fort, cut-timber palisade, Trench Line with timber lining | sawn timber, lime-mortared stone | lime, nails: tender calls | 200 to 600 |
| F4 (beyond rung 4) | Star Fort (earth and brick or stone) | earth, brick or stone, a lot of labour | tools, brick or lime | 600 to 2,000 |
| F5 (relearned or traded) | Concrete bunker and casemate | cement, steel rods | cement, steel, a mould frame: trade or relearned from ruins | 300 to 1,000 |

Honest check: these labour numbers are guesses. The shelter note says labour jumps about 20 times from rung 1 to 2, and that rung 4 is limited by what the island cannot make. A fort follows the same break: stone is limited by labour, lime and concrete by trade. The shelter ladder model (`ecosystem/shelter_ladder.py`) gives about 6 people doing 4.2 person-days a day, so a wall that costs 150 person-days is about a month of building. (Medium on the link, Low on the number.)

### 6.9 How a player picks a style

A style is a set of parts plus rules. The rules are a few lines each:
- Which parts it may use (palisade piece, bank piece, stone wall piece, tower, bastion point, trench bay, casemate, boom).
- Which material it is made of (the hit point and factor table).
- Which weapon it unlocks (the weapon ladder).
- Its special rule (for example Earth and Timber: repair is cheap and twice as fast, but fire spreads; Star Fort: neighbouring parts cover each other (flank fire bonus); Trench Line: a part dug into a slope counts as half as tall to flat fire; Concrete: very strong but a failed part cannot be repaired, only replaced).
The player picks at the start of a skirmish or unlocks as the ladder rises. An "era pack" is a fixed set (section 7.4).

## 7 Play modes

### 7.1 Skirmish (two sides, build phase then fire phase)

- **Build phase** (say 3 to 5 minutes real time, or a number of build points). Each side has the same budget and its own region of the map. Place parts, dig, choose a weapon.
- **Fire phase.** Turn by turn or real time with reload. Each side fires at the other's bunker (the thing you protect). First to zero hit points wins. Nothing gory: the "units" are structures only. No people are shown getting hurt. The bunker is a store, a lighthouse, a flag, a cairn. The lesson of the older toy-war game (`games/baguettes-and-ballistics`): clear numbers, chunky parts and a visible wreck felt good. See section 9.
- **Scoring that rewards clever shape, not just more guns.** Score = base for winning + bonus for hit points left (survival) + bonus for efficiency (budget not spent) + shape bonus (for example the number of enemy shots absorbed or deflected by lean-back faces, flanking cover given and dead ground removed, the use of hill and reverse slope) - penalty for repair wasted. Cap the number of guns so extra guns do not pay. This makes the bastion, the ridge and the trench the clever picks.
- **Fairness.** Equal budget, mirrored or seeded map, wind shown to both. Two players on one screen, two devices, or an AR table (section 7.5).
- **Does damage last?** Open question for Harry. A friendly default: no, the board resets, but the "ruin" can be kept as a place to visit (7.3).

### 7.2 Co-op: a storm or raiders arriving by sea

- Both players build one shared fort. Waves of boats or a big storm arrive. The storm damages walls and the sea breaks small boats. The "raiders" are boats with crews you never see hurt: a boat that is wrecked or turned back just leaves. Win by holding the lamp or harbour until dawn.
- Uses the same parts and rules. It also fits the ecosystem (storm states from `weather.js`).
- Raiders as an in-world event only if the story justifies it (an open question): tone rails say violence only when justified by the story.

### 7.3 Quiet builder mode: forts as something to build and visit

- Forts are just something to build, repair and walk round (this is what Harry did with his parents). Heritage visitors come to see a fort you have restored (a hopeful loop). A "plaque" card gives the style's story in a line.
- **Ruins as exploration.** Old forts found half buried: a bank under turf, a fallen tower, a flooded trench. Repair with the parts method: lift stones back to their sockets, dig out the ditch, re-lay the planks. Each repair unlocks a story card and perhaps a new style. This ties to era 6 (Remembering) in EVOLUTION_TREE.md, and the old-world slots per seed.
- No fighting at all. This might be the first thing to build, because it needs no balancing.

### 7.4 Historical-style "era pack"

- A fixed bundle: one style, one weapon, a period map, a short story card, a palette. For example "Hill Fort Pack", "Star Fort Pack", "Coastal Tower Pack", "Trench Pack", "Concrete Pack". Play as that style in skirmish or co-op.
- Packs are data: part list, tables, and a story card. No new code once the engine reads the tables.

### 7.5 One screen, two devices, AR

| Mode | One screen | Two devices | AR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skirmish | Split table: each side's half; take turns in the fire phase | Each device shows its own half; the other side's fort is hidden until it is hit (fog of war). Sync: parts, shots, wind seed (small messages) | Table view of the island, hold up the tablet to see the fort. Shots as arcs. Reuse the older game's AR notes |
| Co-op | Share the screen, one builds, one aims | Each takes a role | Walk round the fort |
| Builder | One person | Visit each other's islands | Best of all: a miniature fort you walk round |

Lessons from the older game (`games/baguettes-and-ballistics`): readable cost numbers, a build ghost that turns green or red, clear drivable lanes, chunky toy-like parts, a "kettle click" timer that makes the fire phase feel like an event, and wrecks that leave something. Take the lessons only, not the code.

## 8 What the kit would need

### 8.1 Three starter styles (recommended)

1. **Earth and Timber Hill Fort** (ditch, bank, palisade, gate, watch post). Cheapest, rung 1 to 2, fits driftwood and turf, uses the hill, and the parts are simple boxes you can repair with planks (like the jetty).
2. **Star Fort in earth and stone** (angled bastions, ditch, glacis, ravelin). Harry walked round old forts in France, and bastion forts and their gentle green slopes are the shape most people remember. It is also the best "clever shape" style. Make it small (two or three bastions) so it fits an island.
3. **Trench and Concrete Bunker** (trench bay, dugout, pillbox, casemate, wire, camouflage roof). Teaches digging, the reverse slope and hard shells. Late rung but a strong contrast, and the best place to show camouflage.

Why not a castle or coastal tower first? They are lovely and very drawable, and the round tower with a roof gun ties to the lighthouse. But they add a tall thin silhouette (more triangles) and give less to learn from. Put "Coastal Tower" as the fourth style.

### 8.4 Ties to `repair_physics.js` and the kit

- A wall part is a body with a mass (volume x density), the same as the planks. A socket holds it. A joint (lashing, nail, mortar) has a break load. A shell's hit adds a load. When the load passes the break load, the part comes free and tumbles (stones fall, planks splinter). A loose stone you can lift and place back is already how the jetty plank works.
- Earth is not a rigid part. Treat it as a height field cut and pack (a terrain edit) with a work cost, not a lift.
- Concrete is one big body. A failed piece is one large slab and a heap of small spalls. Repair is "replace whole", for cost.
- The "health factor" per part in `repair_physics.js` (0.8 to 1.05 seeded) is a good way to give each stone its own strength so walls do not fail on a clean line.

### 8.5 Rendering and budget for tablets

- Use the same triangle budgets as the kit: far 300 to 700 triangles, live 1,500 to 4,000, hero 8,000 to 15,000 (KIT_PIECES.md). A fort is many copies of a few parts, so use instancing (one draw call per part type).
- A long wall is 20 to 40 segments of a single mesh. The far level can be one merged strip.
- Damage as texture and mesh swap, not live simulation, except for the few parts in motion. Dust clouds: a small particle sprite pool (24 to 48 sprites), reused.
- Only a handful of parts are live bodies at once (the ones just broken). The rest sleep, as in `repair_physics.js`.
- Shot arcs: draw a thin line of 30 to 60 points, not a mesh. Physics runs at a fixed step, as the toy does (0.02 s steps).
- Test on a real tablet before trusting any number. I have not measured any (see section 11).
